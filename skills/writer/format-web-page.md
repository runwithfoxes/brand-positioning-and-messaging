# How to write a web page

This file is the same for every brand.

An email arrives at somebody. A page waits for them. They arrive sceptical, in a hurry, from
somewhere you did not choose, and they leave at the first moment nothing is holding them.

---

# Instructions for Claude

## The top of the page is the whole job

You have about five seconds before somebody decides whether to keep reading. In those five seconds
they are silently asking five things:

1. What is this?
2. Is it for me?
3. Why should I care?
4. Why you and not the others?
5. Do I believe you?

The headline and the line under it have to answer the first three. The proof beside them answers
the fifth. If the top of the page cannot do that, nothing below it gets read.

### Scoring a headline

Five things, and two of them count double, because those two are where homepage lines actually
fail.

| | Weight | The question |
|---|---|---|
| Clarity | 1 | One read. Do you know what it is and who it is for? |
| **Pull** | **2** | Does it lead with what the reader wants, rather than what the thing is? |
| **Difference** | **2** | Can you hear "unlike the others"? Could only this company have written it? |
| Specificity | 1 | Concrete, or vague adjectives? |
| Voice | 1 | Plain and human, passes the slop rules? |

Score each out of five, double the two in bold, and give the reason beside every score. Above about
21 out of 25 it is ready. Below 15 it needs starting again rather than editing.

⭐ **Score each line cold, against the questions, never against the last one you wrote.** A weak set
makes a mediocre line look good.

## What the rest of the page is for

Three jobs, in this order, because it is the order people think in.

**Trust, at the top.** Proof goes beside the headline, not further down. Waiting to earn trust
later means earning it from somebody who has already gone. Logos, ratings, a number, a named
customer.

**Belief, through the middle.** This is where you make them want it and show them it works.

- **How it works.** Three steps, scannable in a couple of seconds. If the process looks like
  effort, people leave. The point is to answer "how hard is this going to be" before they ask it.
- **What they get out of it.** Outcomes, not features. "Built to last ten years, tested across five
  thousand uses" rather than "high quality materials". Specific is what makes it believed.
- **What is included.** Plainly. Uncertainty does not convert.
- **Proof, layered.** Not one testimonial in a box. Written reviews with real names, ratings, video
  if it exists. Video is harder to fake and people know that.

**Decision, at the bottom.** They are close, and now they are looking for reasons not to.

- **How you compare.** Side by side with the alternatives, honestly. Brands avoid this and it is
  usually a mistake.
- **The questions they are actually worried about.** Five to eight, and every one is a doubt you
  are removing rather than filler. Price, what happens if it goes wrong, whether it works for their
  situation, how to get out of it.
- **The last call**, which should feel different from the one at the top. The top one is
  functional. This one speaks to the outcome.

## Rules that matter more on a page than anywhere else

**One primary action.** A page that asks for three things gets none of them. Secondary links can
exist, but only one thing looks like the button.

**Put the action where intent peaks, not just at the end.** People decide at different points.
A single call to action at the bottom only catches the ones who read everything, and most do not.

**Write for the skimmer as well as the reader.** Roughly seven in ten scan. Headings and the first
line of each section have to carry the argument on their own. Read only the headings back and see
whether the case still stands up.

**Specific beats impressive.** A number, a date, a name. "Ten years" outperforms "long lasting"
every time, and it is easier to write.

**Say what it costs, or say why you cannot.** A page that hides the price teaches people you are
expensive and evasive.

## Before you show it

- The five-second questions answered above the fold.
- The headline scored, with reasons, and the score shown.
- One primary action, repeated where intent peaks.
- The headings alone still make the case.
- Every claim traceable to the brand's proof file.
- Slop rules run, both passes.
- Claims ledger shown.
- Scored against the messaging framework, honestly.

## Two last checks on any headline

From the course's module on positioning and messaging for a website.

**The logo test.** If a competitor could put their logo on your headline and it would still be
true, rewrite it. The check, in four steps, is the course's own and has no outside source. Open five
competitor homepages. For each, write down the headline, the sub-line and the first three claims.
Count the words that appear on three or more of them. Then read your own draft against that list.

There are two tests with an actual source. Jakob Nielsen's competitor-tagline test: collect your
tagline and the taglines of your three strongest competitors, take the names off, and see whether
anyone can tell which company does what. Ask people outside your business too. His second test is
the opposite test: rewrite your main copy to say the reverse. If no company would ever say the
reverse (nobody advertises "buggy, fragmented, inefficient, and expensive"), then your copy tells
the visitor nothing (Nielsen Norman Group, "Tagline Blues: What's the Site About?", 21 Jul 2001,
https://www.nngroup.com/articles/tagline-blues-whats-the-site-about/). Grade: one expert's column
from 2001. It reports no participants. It is a sound test because it needs none, not because a study
proved it.

For a headline:
1. Does it say what you do and for whom in plain words?
2. Would it still be true with a competitor's logo on it? If so, rewrite.
3. Would anyone ever say the opposite? If not, it says nothing.
4. Is the key word in the first three words?
5. Does it fit in two lines at phone width?
6. Is there a number, name or place that could replace an adjective?

For a section:
1. One idea. One job. Can you say it in a sentence?
2. Does it open with the point, then the support?
3. Is there proof within one screen of the claim?
4. Is it written about the reader, not about us?
5. Is there a single next step?
6. Does it still make sense read on a phone with only the headings visible?
